What I'm reading

Canoeing the Mountains

Tod Bolsinger
It's a book about church leadership. I keep reading it as a book about engineering leadership after AI.

Bolsinger's premise is that Lewis and Clark set out to canoe to the Pacific and found the Rocky Mountains in the way. The canoes were excellent. They were also the wrong tool. What happens when everything that took you up to this point no longer adds value?

In the past, coding was leading. Writing the hard part yourself, being the person who could unblock anything — that was how engineering leaders earned the room. Post-AI, those tools are available to everyone. What happens to your processes, your team dynamics, and your sense of self-worth when the skills you used to pride yourself on no longer drive value in the same way?

The book is blunt about the cost of that. When the old map stops working, something in you has to die before anything new gets built, and replacing your own paradigm is both genuinely painful and completely necessary. Most organizations don't move until they feel that. I've come to think part of my job is naming it early enough that it's a decision instead of an emergency.

Two other things I've underlined. First: before people follow you off the map, you have to have demonstrated competence on it — which is a large part of why I still write code. Second: in uncharted territory, trust is the whole currency, and if it's lost the trip is over. That's why this work is measured in days spent alongside your team rather than decks delivered to them.

FAQ

Things people usually ask me.

How is this different from an advisor who takes calls?

I believe transformational leadership requires trust and attention. That means sitting in your team planning meetings, understanding team dynamics, and shepherding the team to come up with their own answers. Guiding your people to their own transformation at a rate they can absorb.

How much of your time would we actually get?

Usually two to four days a month — a standing weekly block plus whatever comes up in between. Enough to steer things, not so much that I become the bottleneck.

We already have a CTO. Where would you fit?

Within their structure, not above or around them. Most CTOs at this size are already carrying platform, security, hiring, and team organization. I come inside that structure to provide process transformation through continuous improvement.

Could you work with our outsourced team?

Often that's the reason I'm called. I've chosen vendors, run the relationship end to end, and rebuilt the review and acceptance habits that make an outside team productive rather than expensive.

What if we end up wanting someone full time?

That happens, and it's fine. I've covered during searches and handed off to the permanent hire — sometimes sitting on the panel that picked them.

How does this work, practically?

A flat monthly rate rather than hourly billing, and thirty days notice on either side. What it costs depends on cadence and scope, so it is easiest to sort out on a call.
