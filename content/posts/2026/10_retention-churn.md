More sales only treads water if you can't hold on to the customers you already have.

I'm working with an independent insurance agency right now, and their real growth problem isn't acquisition. It's retention. Renewals compound. Lose the customer and that stream just stops. They spent real time and money winning that person. Losing them to a forgotten email is the expensive kind of churn - the kind where nobody decided to lose them.

## Churn starts in the inbox

It rarely looks like a cancellation. It looks like this:

- "I'd like to evaluate my renewal. Can you add or drop someone from my plan?"
- "Just following up on the email I sent last week."
- "Can someone call me back about my coverage?"

Nobody ignores these on purpose. The inbox is busy, the email gets buried, a week goes by. But to the customer, a week of silence is an answer. By the time a competitor calls, the decision is already half made.

The agency's bottleneck isn't effort. It's attention. Somebody has to see the right email at the right moment, and humans are bad at that when the inbox never stops.

## Start with the cheap win

The temptation is to reach for a big platform. We didn't. The first step is putting a few extra sets of eyes on the inbox with Power Automate:

1. Scan incoming mail for high-intent signals: renewal questions, plan changes, follow-ups, frustration
2. Flag those messages for follow-up so they can't blend into the pile
3. Escalate automatically if nobody has replied after a set amount of time

No new software for the team to learn, and no migration. It works inside the mailbox they already live in. This is the same lesson I learned building an AI agent for health-sharing members: meet people where they already work.

## Let the team keep experimenting

The agency built their own CRM with an AI app builder, and they did a genuinely good job of it. I'm not here to replace it. The experimenting is how they find out what they actually need.

If you lock a system down too early, you freeze your guesses. Wait too long and you scale chaos. So for now the plan is to keep enabling experimentation, and once the team has something they've adopted and like, decide deliberately whether to harden it or move off the platform.

## Growth by acquisition needs written-down process

This agency grows by acquiring books of business, and every acquisition brings new people who need to get up to speed. Today, most of their process lives in a few people's heads. That works until it doesn't.

This is the part of the work that looks most like a fractional COO: sitting with the team, drafting the standard operating procedures, and writing down why things are done the way they are, so a new hire doesn't have to reconstruct it from scratch.

## Retention is a process problem first

Technology helps, but the fix started with a simple question: what would we hate to miss? Answer that, build the smallest thing that catches it, and let the rest of the process grow around it.

If retention is the quiet drag on your growth and follow-up can't keep up, I'd be glad to compare notes.
